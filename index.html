João Dimas

I study corporate finance and entrepreneurship, with a focus on the economics of technology—particularly artificial intelligence.

My research examines how venture capital shapes software development, how AI changes the trade-offs between productivity and software quality, and how cybersecurity vulnerabilities affect firm performance. I combine source-code and firm-level data to study these questions.

I received my PhD in Finance from Washington University in St. Louis (Olin Business School) in 2026, advised by Todd Gormley. My background in software engineering and industry research informs my work.

Venture Capital and Engineering Quality: Evidence from Software Startups

with Labros Pnevmatikos (third-year PhD student)

First Prize, Buchan Prize Paper Competition, Olin Business School.

Presented at the 12th Annual USC Marshall PhD Conference in Finance, 2026.

  • Paper (PDF)
  • SSRN
Abstract for Venture Capital and Engineering Quality: Evidence from Software Startups
We document a new dimension of venture capital (VC) professionalization: the way startups build and maintain software. Code quality is difficult to measure across firms because source code is usually proprietary. We apply a common framework to monthly snapshots of public source code from 569 firms, covering 13.2 billion lines, and estimate technical debt in dollars as the cost of correcting problems that make code harder to maintain. Following first institutional VC entry, funded firms’ critical code issues per line decline by 15% and technical debt per line by 12% of their pre-funding averages, relative to firms without VC backing. These gains persist after matching on pre-funding growth in employment and codebase size. Later rounds bring substantial hiring without detectable quality gains. Descriptions accompanying 5.6 million code contributions reveal more systematic documentation and organization of development work after first entry. Engineers already active before financing become less likely to introduce bugs. Together, the findings are consistent with VC professionalization extending beyond governance and leadership to the engineering work of startups’ existing teams.

AI and Technical Debt: Evidence from 4 Billion Lines of Code

with Eddie Lu and Labros Pnevmatikos

  • Paper (PDF)
Abstract for AI and Technical Debt: Evidence from 4 Billion Lines of Code
We examine whether generative artificial intelligence (AI) speeds up software development at the expense of code quality. Our novel monthly panel tracks 1,459 corporate public software projects from 2022 to 2026, covering 3.9 billion lines of source code. An industry-standard tool identifies maintainability issues, bugs, and security vulnerabilities in each monthly snapshot of the code. We convert its estimated repair time into a dollar measure of technical debt, the future cost of resolving maintainability issues. We compare code quality changes around first detected AI use with changes at comparable projects where we have not yet detected AI use. Contrary to concerns about “AI slop,” total issues per line decline by 1.4%, bugs per line by 2.1%, and technical debt per line by 0.7% of their averages before detection. Quality gains concentrate in projects with weaker development practices before AI use. We also find that issues per unit of software functionality decline relative to comparison projects. Together, the findings link detected AI use to faster growth in firms’ software assets and a lower engineering burden relative to both code volume and software features.

Patch Now, Pay Later? Cybersecurity Vulnerabilities and Technical Debt

with Labros Pnevmatikos

  • Paper (PDF)
  • SSRN
Abstract for Patch Now, Pay Later? Cybersecurity Vulnerabilities and Technical Debt
We study how cybersecurity vulnerabilities affect firm performance. We analyze 610 open-core software firms from 2010 to 2025, comparing firms affected by vulnerability discoveries with unaffected firms. These discoveries concern third-party dependency packages, making their timing plausibly exogenous to the firms using them. Exposed firms experience approximately 10% slower headcount growth relative to their pre-treatment average and a 1.5 percentage point decline in acquisition probability. Commit-level evidence reveals a crowding-out mechanism: the share of non-remediation development activity falls by 2.8 percentage points, and fix-related effort remains elevated for twelve months, while feature-related activity declines at longer horizons. Technical debt and cognitive complexity grow approximately 20 and 24 percentage points faster, respectively. Together, the findings suggest that cybersecurity vulnerabilities can disrupt firm growth and place persistent demands on engineering resources.

Acquisition Prospects and Startup Strategy

with Anushri Jain and Labros Pnevmatikos

Abstract for Acquisition Prospects and Startup Strategy
Acquisitions are an important exit channel for entrepreneurial firms. We examine how startups adjust product-development and hiring strategies when the likelihood of acquisition declines. Our analysis asks whether reduced acquisition prospects cause startups to invest more heavily in building durable standalone businesses and how these changes affect the composition of their workforce and product-development choices.

Hedge or Speculation? Evidence from Brazil

with Gustavo Cortes, Sergio Leao and Alexandre Pecora

Abstract for Hedge or Speculation? Evidence from Brazil

Do non-financial firms simply hedge and hold, or do they actively manage their derivative positions over time in response to changing market conditions? We find clear evidence of the latter: Brazilian firms frequently adjust their positions as conditions shift, rather than passively maintaining a hedge tied to their operating exposure.

We then examine what drives these adjustments. Two forces appear to be at work: firms respond to the recent performance of their own derivative positions, consistent with the “house money effect” of Thaler and Johnson (1990), and to prevailing market pricing, timing their positions to take advantage of favorable currency-market conditions. Both forces are present, but the first dominates, suggesting an agency problem in corporate hedging. We also document that firms with opposite underlying currency exposures respond quite differently to the same market conditions. These patterns have direct implications for how shareholders, investors, and regulators should think about corporate hedging programs.

A White Box Framework for Estimating Long-Term Experimental Impact via Short-Term Metrics

with Wen Qiang Toh, Yidi Wang and Wenjing Zheng

ACM KDD 4th End to End Customer Journey Optimization Workshop, 2025

Abstract for A White Box Framework for Estimating Long-Term Experimental Impact via Short-Term Metrics
We develop a framework for estimating long-term experimental effects from short-term metrics while making the underlying causal mechanisms explicit. The framework connects short-term measures to a long-term outcome through its key drivers, validates estimates using both pseudo-experiments and actual experiments, and addresses implementation on an experimentation platform.
