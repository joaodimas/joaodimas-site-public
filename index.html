João Dimas

I study corporate finance and entrepreneurship, with a focus on the economics of technology—particularly artificial intelligence.

My research examines how venture capital shapes software development, how AI changes the trade-offs between productivity and software quality, and how cybersecurity vulnerabilities affect firm performance. I combine source-code and firm-level data to study these questions.

I received my PhD in Finance from Washington University in St. Louis (Olin Business School) in 2026, advised by Todd Gormley. My background in software engineering and industry research informs my work.

Venture Capital and Engineering Quality: Evidence from Software Startups

with Lampros Pnevmatikos (third-year PhD student)

Buchan Prize, 1st place (best PhD student-written paper).

Presented at the 12th Annual USC Marshall PhD Conference in Finance, 2026.

  • Paper (PDF)
  • SSRN
Abstract for Venture Capital and Engineering Quality: Evidence from Software Startups
We investigate a novel channel of venture capital value creation: the practices through which startups build and maintain their technology. We construct a new panel of 569 firms using 60,000 full codebase snapshots. We compare changes in engineering quality following first VC investment with changes at similar firms that remain without VC backing. We also construct a dollar-denominated measure of technical debt, the estimated maintenance liability embedded in software. Relative to codebase size, serious deviations from coding best practices decline by 15% and technical debt by 12%. These improvements accompany substantial codebase growth. The decline in the frequency of serious coding problems persists after matching on pre-financing growth, and quality gains extend to firms already generating revenue. In contrast, a placebo test around later financing rounds shows no evidence of quality improvement. We also analyze 4.8 million code contributions (commits). After first VC investment, documented peer review and formal issue tracking become more common, off-hours work declines, and existing team members become less likely to introduce defective code. The findings suggest that VC professionalization extends to the engineering practices through which teams build and maintain their products.

Cybersecurity Vulnerabilities and Firm Performance

with Lampros Pnevmatikos

  • Paper (PDF)
  • SSRN
Abstract for Cybersecurity Vulnerabilities and Firm Performance
We study how cybersecurity vulnerabilities affect firm performance. We analyze 551 open-core software firms from 2010 to 2025, comparing firms affected by vulnerability discoveries with unaffected firms. These discoveries concern third-party dependency packages, making their timing plausibly exogenous to the firms using them. Exposed firms experience approximately 10% slower headcount growth relative to their pre-treatment average and a 1.7 percentage point decline in acquisition probability. Developer activity shifts toward remediation: the share of non-remediation work falls by 2.8 percentage points, while elevated fix-related activity persists for twelve months. These changes accompany faster accumulation of technical debt and cognitive complexity, which grow approximately 20 and 16–21 percentage points faster, respectively. Together, the findings suggest that cybersecurity vulnerabilities can disrupt firm growth and place persistent demands on engineering resources.

AI and Technical Debt: Evidence from 3 Billion Lines of Code

with Eddie Lu and Lampros Pnevmatikos

Abstract for AI and Technical Debt: Evidence from 3 Billion Lines of Code
Does artificial intelligence make software cheaper to build but more expensive to maintain? We study how advances in AI coding capability change engineering quality. We construct a new panel of a million full codebase snapshots, each capturing the complete software at a particular point in time. These data let us measure technical debt—the estimated maintenance liability embedded in software—alongside reliability, security, and complexity. We also examine development and review practices to understand how teams incorporate AI-assisted work into their products. The project brings the quality of software capital into the assessment of AI’s economic value: producing code faster is valuable, but so is building technology that remains reliable and economical to maintain.

Acquisition Prospects and Startup Strategy

with Anushri Jain and Lampros Pnevmatikos

Abstract for Acquisition Prospects and Startup Strategy
Acquisitions are an important exit channel for entrepreneurial firms. We examine how startups adjust product-development and hiring strategies when the likelihood of acquisition declines. Our analysis asks whether reduced acquisition prospects cause startups to invest more heavily in building durable standalone businesses and how these changes affect the composition of their workforce and product-development choices.

Hedge or Speculation? Evidence from Brazil

with Gustavo Cortes, Sergio Leao and Alexandre Pecora

Abstract for Hedge or Speculation? Evidence from Brazil

Do non-financial firms simply hedge and hold, or do they actively manage their derivative positions over time in response to changing market conditions? We find clear evidence of the latter: Brazilian firms frequently adjust their positions as conditions shift, rather than passively maintaining a hedge tied to their operating exposure.

We then examine what drives these adjustments. Two forces appear to be at work: firms respond to the recent performance of their own derivative positions, consistent with the “house money effect” of Thaler and Johnson (1990), and to prevailing market pricing, timing their positions to take advantage of favorable currency-market conditions. Both forces are present, but the first dominates, suggesting an agency problem in corporate hedging. We also document that firms with opposite underlying currency exposures respond quite differently to the same market conditions. These patterns have direct implications for how shareholders, investors, and regulators should think about corporate hedging programs.

Abstract for A White Box Framework for Estimating Long-Term Experimental Impact via Short-Term Metrics
We develop a framework for estimating long-term experimental effects from short-term metrics while making the underlying causal mechanisms explicit. The framework connects short-term measures to a long-term outcome through its key drivers, validates estimates using both pseudo-experiments and actual experiments, and addresses implementation on an experimentation platform.
